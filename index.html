01 / The question

What would an eight-year Skubal deal actually buy?

I started with a baseball question, not a contract prediction: if Toronto signed Tarik Skubal for eight seasons beginning in 2027, how much value might the Blue Jays get back?

There are two separate problems here. First, how much will he pitch, and how well? Second, what might that production cost in a free-agent market? Neither question has a single knowable answer.

What this is: an independent, exploratory valuation using publicly available data. What it is not: an MLB team's proprietary projection, medical evaluation, scouting report, or internal financial model.
02 / Data and scope

Start with what I could actually access

I used public Statcast pitch records, MLB game-feed information, FanGraphs pitcher WAR and its public free-agent tracker. I collected the pitch data in seasonal pieces and cached downloads, both to make the work manageable and to avoid repeatedly requesting the same information.

I restricted the main pitch analysis to regular-season games and checked game counts, dates, and duplicates. The historical pitcher data covers 2010 to 2026; the contract tracker covers signing classes 2022 to 2026.

The public sources are useful, but they do not provide everything a team would use: internal medical records, biomechanical assessments, proprietary forecasts, contract negotiations, and detailed risk pricing are all missing.

03 / Pitch profile

Before projecting him, understand the pitcher

I looked at pitch usage, velocity, spin, extension, and movement by season. Then I isolated his starts against AL East opponents. The idea was straightforward: if Toronto is considering a major commitment, I want to know whether the pitch profile and opponent history reveal anything that a season-level WAR number might miss.

These are descriptive checks, not claims that a particular pitch characteristic causes future success. Pitch-level measurements also change with tracking systems, context, and usage.

04 / Rogers Centre

Does the roof change the story?

Because Toronto plays under a retractable roof, I tested whether indoor conditions appeared to affect Skubal's fastball velocity, movement, or effectiveness. I also expanded the analysis to left-handed pitchers at Rogers Centre and tried to compare pitchers observed under both roof conditions.

I moved from raw averages to models controlling for season or pitcher-season, with game-clustered uncertainty where appropriate. I checked sensitivity to individual games and looked at whiffs, rather than stopping at movement differences.

What I took away: I did not find convincing evidence to add a Rogers Centre roof bonus to Skubal's projected value. An apparent movement difference is not enough if it does not translate into a reliable effectiveness advantage.

Roof status required interpretation of game-feed weather descriptions and manual corrections. That classification is a limitation, and these observational comparisons cannot isolate a causal roof effect.

whiff_models <- purrr::map_dfr(
  c("FF", "SI"),
  function(pt) {
    
    dat <- rogers_whiffs |>
      filter(pitch_type == pt) |>
      mutate(whiff = as.integer(whiff))
    
    model <- glm(
      whiff ~ roof_closed + pitcher_season,
      data = dat,
      family = binomial()
    )
    
    robust_vcov <- sandwich::vcovCL(
      model,
      cluster = dat$game_pk,
      type = "HC1"
    )
    
    test <- lmtest::coeftest(
      model,
      vcov. = robust_vcov
    )
    
    estimate <- unname(test["roof_closed", "Estimate"])
    se <- unname(test["roof_closed", "Std. Error"])
    
    tibble(
      pitch_type = pt,
      swings = nrow(dat),
      games = n_distinct(dat$game_pk),
      odds_ratio = exp(estimate),
      lower_95 = exp(estimate - 1.96 * se),
      upper_95 = exp(estimate + 1.96 * se),
      p_value = test["roof_closed", "Pr(>|z|)"]
    )
  }
)

05 / 2026 check

Look for changes inside the most recent season

I split the 2026 pitch data into pre- and post-injured-list periods and compared velocity, movement, spin, extension, release position, and pitch mix. I then examined game-by-game trends and whether fastball and sinker movement changed together.

I did this because a single full-season average can hide meaningful changes. But a pre/post split is not a medical diagnosis, and a time trend alone cannot identify its cause. I kept these checks as context rather than using them to invent an injury penalty.

07 / Pandemic sensitivity

What about the shortened 2020 season?

Every historical follow-up window crossed the pandemic-shortened 2020 season. That makes raw eight-year totals a little awkward to compare with a hypothetical run of eight full seasons.

I tried a simple sensitivity check: replace 2020 WAR with the average of each pitcher's 2019 and 2021 WAR, including zero for absent seasons. This moved the cohort mean from 12.4 to approximately 13.4 WAR.

Important: this is a heuristic counterfactual, not an estimate of what pitchers definitely would have produced in a full 2020 season. It is useful for testing sensitivity, not for rewriting history.

08 / Performance forecast

Turn history into an eight-year estimate

I modeled eight-year future WAR using cumulative WAR at ages 27 to 29. Skubal entered the calculation with 17.96 WAR over 2024 to 2026. Adding age-29 WAR or innings pitched did not meaningfully improve the model in the checks I ran, so I retained the simpler specification.

That interval matters more than the extra decimal places in the point estimate. The model explains only a portion of historical variation, and pitching careers are volatile.

I allocated the eight-year total across ages using the historical mean aging pattern. Those annual numbers are an allocation of the overall forecast, not eight independently estimated seasonal forecasts.

09 / Free-agent market

Performance is only half the problem

To estimate what teams actually pay, I collected public starting-pitcher contracts from five signing classes (2022 to 2026). Across the full tracker, 197 of 249 pitcher entries had a reported contract total; the smaller salary-regression dataset contained 71 qualifying observations.

I separated total guarantees or reported totals from annual average value, and checked cases where the tracker-reported AAV differed from total divided by years. Options, deferrals, and reporting conventions can complicate those figures. I used nominal total divided by years for a consistent comparison, but that is not necessarily economic present value.

For elite starters, the public comparisons show substantial differences in salary even among pitchers with strong recent WAR. That is a warning against simply multiplying past WAR by a dollar figure.

10 / Model checks

I tested the models instead of trusting the first answer

The initial salary regression used signing year, previous-season WAR, age, and contract length. I inspected influential contracts, reran the model on productive starters, tested alternative minimum-WAR thresholds, and used leave-one-out cross-validation.

Then I reconstructed three-year WAR for the 71-contract sample, explicitly filling verified non-MLB seasons with zero. A model using three-year WAR was not clearly better than one using the preceding season alone.

That is a near tie, not a decisive win. The models also struggled on several expensive, high-profile contracts. Cross-validation across this historical sample does not guarantee accuracy for a player as unusual as Skubal.

11 / Market estimates

Two models, two very different prices

When I supplied Skubal's profile and an eight-year term, the one-year WAR model suggested roughly $451M. The three-year WAR model suggested roughly $595M. Their 95% prediction intervals were extraordinarily wide.

One-year model · 8 years$451M
Three-year model · 8 years$595M

These numbers are exploratory model outputs, not recommended bids. Skubal's 17.96 three-year WAR exceeds the highest three-year WAR in the salary-model sample (14.5). The $595M result especially depends on extending a fitted relationship beyond the observed performance range.

For context, the nine elite comparable contracts in the study ranged from $110M to $218M. Differences in years, signing dates, health, and player circumstances mean that range is not a direct Skubal price quote.

13 / Bayesian extension

A Bayesian look at the uncertainty

The original aging model gave me a point estimate and a very wide prediction interval. I wanted to take that one step further. Rather than use only 24.6 WAR in the contract math, I added a Bayesian model that produces a distribution of possible eight-year outcomes.

I used the same historical comparison group and recent three-year WAR predictor, but fitted a Student-t Bayesian regression with regularizing priors. The Student-t likelihood gives unusually high or low career outcomes some room without letting them dominate quite as easily. I chose broad priors and made them explicit in the R script so they can be challenged and tested.

There is an important difference between uncertainty about the average outcome and uncertainty about one pitcher. For the financial simulation, I use posterior predictive draws for an individual pitcher. Each draw is then allocated across the eight seasons and valued under the same contract assumptions. This would let me estimate, for example, how often a $400 million contract produces positive modeled surplus under a particular starting price per WAR and annual growth rate.

This is a new modeling extension with reproducible R code, not a set of results I have already validated. I have not published a probability of positive surplus because the model still needs to be run, checked for convergence, tested against alternative priors, and assessed for predictive performance. A precise-looking percentage would not be useful before those checks.

There are limitations here too. Bayesian methods do not create new information. I still have the same small, selected historical cohort. My prior choices and the assumed likelihood affect the result, and I am still using a fixed annual WAR shape when I translate an eight-year total into discounted value. This is not a hierarchical model of each season of a pitcher's career, and it does not independently validate the aging assumptions.

For me, the benefit is that the Bayesian approach makes the uncertainty part of the valuation itself. It changes the question from “What is the surplus at one projected WAR total?” to “How often does this contract work under the model and assumptions I chose?” That is a better question, as long as I remember what the probability does and does not mean.

14 / Limitations

What this analysis can and cannot tell us

I approached this project using publicly available Statcast, FanGraphs, and MLB data. That gave me plenty to work with, but I still had to make choices about what to compare, how to build the models, and how much confidence to put in the results. Here are the main limitations I kept in mind.

The historical comparisons are not perfect

I started by identifying pitchers who were roughly the same age as Skubal and had similar recent performance. I wanted to see what happened to them over the following eight seasons. No two pitchers are exactly alike, though, and the final group included just 31 pitchers. That gives me a useful historical reference, not a guarantee that Skubal will follow the same path.

The 2020 season complicated things

Every pitcher in the historical comparison group had 2020 in the eight-year follow-up period. Since that season was shortened, I tested an adjustment using the surrounding seasons. That helped me see how sensitive the results were to 2020, but the adjusted numbers are estimates, not performance that actually happened.

The aging model has a lot of uncertainty

My model projected about 24.6 WAR over eight seasons, but the 95% prediction interval ran from roughly 2.7 to 46.6 WAR. That is a pretty substantial range. Recent performance tells us something about future production, but clearly not everything. I tried additional variables and different models, and they did not consistently improve the predictions.

The salary models had limited information

I paired publicly reported free-agent contracts with pitchers' recent performance. The two salary models performed similarly when tested against historical contracts, yet estimated very different eight-year prices for Skubal: about $451 million and $595 million. His three-year WAR was also higher than anything in that model's training data, so I was asking it to predict beyond what it had observed. That makes both figures worth treating cautiously.

The Rogers Centre analysis was exploratory

I wanted to see whether pitching conditions at Rogers Centre might matter, especially with the roof open or closed. Separating those conditions from differences between pitchers, seasons, and games is difficult. I used comparisons and statistical controls to explore the question, but the results show associations, not proof that the roof caused a change.

The financial results depend on my assumptions

To connect projected WAR with contract value, I used an illustrative $10 million per WAR and a 5% discount rate. Those were assumptions I chose for the scenarios, not values established by the data. Change those assumptions and the financial results change too. The comparisons help show how the numbers respond, rather than identifying one exact contract value.

What I take away from all of this

I did not start this project expecting one perfect answer, and I do not think the results provide one. I wanted to use the public information I could access, test different approaches, see what held up, and understand where the uncertainty came from. Some approaches worked better than others. Some results raised more questions than they answered. That is part of the process. For me, the value is knowing what the data supports, what it does not, and what I would want to investigate next.

15 / The answer

So, would I sign him?

Skubal has earned the conversation. His recent production is exceptional, and the historical model suggests meaningful value in the first half of an eight-year contract. But the uncertainty around eight-year pitching outcomes is enormous.

Under the assumptions I tested, I would not justify a guaranteed eight-year $400M-plus contract using projected regular-season WAR alone. That is not the same as saying Toronto should never make the deal. The decision could look different with a higher defensible market value per WAR, a different performance projection, postseason considerations, or information I cannot access.

My final takeaway: this project is less about finding the one correct number than about showing what has to be true for a huge contract to make sense, and how much uncertainty remains even after doing the math.

That is the value of the exercise: a transparent starting point for a baseball conversation, not a substitute for the work an MLB front office does behind closed doors.
